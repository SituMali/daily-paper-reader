Randomization Inference for Matched Pairs with
Binary Outcomes
Bob Wilson1,∗
1Marketing Data Science at Meta
∗Work done at Meta
We give an exact randomization-based confidence set for the average treatment effect (ATE) in
matched-pair studies with a binary outcome, requiring neither monotonicity nor any distributional
assumption beyond the within-pair coin flip. At its core is an analytic solution to the worst-case
allocation of attributable effects: two binomial-symmetry lemmas identify the pattern hardest to
reject as a single boundary corner, so testing null hypotheses needs no integer program and no
numerical search. Inverting the test via binary search yields a prediction set for the attributable
effect in O(log S) Binomial tail calculations; the Bonferroni proposition of Rigdon and Hudgens (2015)
produces the ATE confidence set at the same computational cost. The same corner extends without
further machinery to a sensitivity analysis for matched observational studies under Rosenbaum’s Γ-
model. A simple formula for the design sensitivity illuminates when an observational study can hope
to provide evidence for an effect.

Introduction
Matched-pair designs are used for both experimental and observational causal inference.
Units may be
paired as a variance reduction strategy, with one unit from each pair randomly selected for treatment. In an
observational study, treated units may be matched to controls on the basis of observed covariates to reduce
selection bias. Inference about a treatment effect in such a design ought to rest on no more than the structure
imposed by the assignment mechanism, whether imposed by the experiment or assumed in the matching.
When the hypothesis of constant treatment effects is reasonable, we may use the Hodges-Lehmann method
to obtain a point estimate (Hodges and Lehmann, 1963; Rosenbaum, 1993). For a binary outcome that
hypothesis is implausible, since a constant nonzero effect forces every untreated unit to a single common
counterfactual value, incompatible with anything but a perfectly homogeneous population.
Rosenbaum
(2001) introduced the notion of an attributable effect as an effect size identified by randomization. It is the
number of successes caused by treatment among the treated, A1, and its mirror A0 for the untreated, the
number of control failures that would have been successes under treatment.
Rosenbaum (2001) built exact prediction sets for A1 in the unstratified case by inverting Fisher’s exact test
on a discrete pivot, under the monotonicity assumption that treatment never prevents a success. Rosenbaum
(2002) extended attributable effects to matched observational studies, giving exact randomization inference
together with a large-sample approximation via asymptotic separability, both under monotonicity. Rigdon
and Hudgens (2015) dropped monotonicity in the unstratified randomized experiment and combined A1 with
A0 via Bonferroni into a confidence set for the average treatment effect; their procedure is fast because each
prediction set inverts a single hypergeometric. They consider stratified problems (including matched pairs)
only briefly, but propose a generic integer programming solution impractical in large sample sizes.
The computational diﬀiculty of exact randomization inference for binary outcomes has drawn recent attention.
Aronow et al. (2025) reduce the computational cost of the unstratified interval of Rigdon and Hudgens (2015)
to O(n log n) permutation tests in balanced designs, but leave the stratified case explicitly open. Li et al.
(2025) treat the stratified binary ATE directly, obtaining exact and conservative intervals by maximizing a

This note gives an analytic solution to the worst-case allocation for matched pairs, and builds the full
attributable-effect and sensitivity machinery around it. Our contributions are:
1. A closed-form worst-case pattern. Two binomial-symmetry lemmas (§4) identify the pattern of treat-
ment effects hardest to reject among the combinatorial number of patterns consistent with a hypothe-
sized A1 = a0, as the unique boundary corner of a two-dimensional integer rectangle. The worst-case
test is a single Binomial tail calculation, with no integer program and no numerical search.
2. A point estimate for the ATE matching the textbook McNemar formula, but motivated by random
assignment rather than sampling arguments.
3. An O(log S) ATE confidence set without monotonicity. Sweeping a0 inverts the test in O(log S) Binomial
tail calculations; the mirror procedure handles A0, and the Bonferroni combination of Rigdon and
Hudgens (2015) delivers the ATE set at the same computational cost, assuming no more than the
within-pair coin flip. A continuity-corrected Gaussian approximation (§5) delivers the endpoints and
the ATE interval in closed form.
4. A sensitivity analysis. The construction extends to matched observational studies via Rosenbaum’s Γ-
model at no additional computational cost (§7), including adjusted p-values, closed-form bounds on
the Hodges-Lehmann point estimate, and expanded confidence intervals.
5. A simple formula for the design sensitivity of a matched pairs study. The design sensitivity tells us the
threshold of “hidden biases” beyond which the null hypothesis of no net treatment effect cannot be
rejected at any sample size, playing a similar role in observational studies that power analysis plays in
experiment design.

McNemar’s test and equivalence classes.
We use McNemar’s test for matched pairs with binary outcomes.
Consider the pairs of control potential outcomes (rCs1, rCs2) (which includes the counterfactual outcomes for
treated units, as implied by the null hypothesis). Some of these pairs consist of two successes or two failures;
these are concordant pairs. Other pairs consist of one success and one failure, the discordant pairs. In an
experiment (or a strongly ignorable observational study), the unit selected for treatment from a discordant
pair is equally likely to have a success or failure as the control outcome. The number of treated units among

Example
Suppose we have a controlled experiment involving S = 1000 pairs, with one unit in each pair
selected at random for treatment. In S00 = 800 of the pairs, neither unit experienced a successful outcome;
in S11 = 100 of the pairs, both units did. In S10 = 70 of the pairs, the treated unit experienced a success
while the control unit did not. In the remaining S01 = 30 pairs, the control unit experienced a success while
the treated unit did not. We observe the Sjk counts.
Suppose we wish to test H0 : A1 ≤0, with a0 = 0. Rejecting this null hypothesis would constitute evidence
the net treatment effect was positive. Then δ0 = 70 −30 −0 = 40. A hypothesis τ 0 corresponds to a claim
about how many of the Sjk pairs would be discordant if we observed the control outcomes for treated units.
A particular τ 0 might correspond to m00 = 400, m11 = 50, m10 = 35, and m01 = 15; that is, half the pairs
in each category would be discordant according to the control outcomes. Then m+0 = 435 and m+1 = 65.
This pattern is consistent with H0, since the realized A1 = −330 ≤0 (equivalently, since 435 −65 ≥40) and
so it is one of the patterns we must test if we hope to reject H0.

• Inferential target and coverage. McNemar targets the marginal probabilities of a population, real or

• Randomization inference analyzes potential outcomes. A sharp null fixes the unit-level effects τ 0
si and
so imputes the missing control outcomes for the treated units.
When asserting a net effect of a0
(and assuming monotonic treatment effects), the worst-case pattern posits a0 treated successes whose
counterfactual was a failure. Each such imputation turns a would-be concordant pair discordant: the
reference distribution is built on the null-implied discordant count S01 + S10 + a0. The population null
instead constrains only the marginal parameter ∆, and because ∑

to the observed concordant cells, it can rearrange discordant pairs but never create them; its variance
stays near the observed S01 + S10 for every ∆0. The two coincide at a0 = 0 and diverge toward the
endpoints. This is a design-based feature, not a choice of variance estimator.
• The role of monotonicity. The count S01+S10+a0 above assumes monotonic treatment effects (τsi ≥0).
Relaxing monotonicity lets a prevented success live in a concordant pair, so the worst-case denominator
inflates further, to 2b⋆+ δ0. In our example, this roughly doubles the binding box ceiling (300 −a0
versus 100 + a0).
• Bonferroni adjustment. Combining the prediction sets for A1 and A0 requires using z1−α/4 instead of
z1−α/2, inflating confidence widths by about 19% in this example. Yet using z1−α/2 in (12) sabotages
the coverage of our interval.
• Width. The three effects multiply. At a common 90% level, the null acting on potential outcomes
inflates the half-width by about 20%, relaxing monotonicity by a further ≈46%, and the Bonferroni
upgrade by another ≈19%, so our half-width (0.0339) is about 1.20 × 1.46 × 1.19 ≈2.1 times as wide

The sensitivity value.
While the amount of hidden bias is typically unknown, we may calculate the point at
which we begin to question a study’s conclusions. This occurs at the smallest Γ at which the study fails to
reject the null of no positive net effect at level α. Call this threshold Γ• the sensitivity value (Zhao, 2019).
Debates over study conclusions can then focus on whether the amount of hidden bias is below or above
this threshold, how we might detect such biases, and how we might design studies to have correspondingly
larger thresholds. A finding with Γ• well above 1 is robust to substantial unmeasured confounding; Γ• close
to 1 would be cause for concern in an observational study, because rarely could we be so confident in our
adjustment strategy.
For the running example, testing H0 : ATE ≤0 at α = 0.10, the sensitivity value is Γ• ≈1.03.

effect size we hope to detect, telling us the sample size we need. Design sensitivity serves a similar role in
observational studies, telling us how thorough our adjustment strategy needs to be.
Based on a relative effect size we hope to detect, we must apply our domain expertise and ensure we measure
the most important confounders. If we lack such ability, there is little hope of success. We may employ
quasi-experimental devices to rule out the possibility of large hidden biases (Rosenbaum, 1989, 2015a).
This discussion demonstrates the hopelessness of measuring small relative effect sizes in an observational
study. No matter our domain expertise, rarely could we be confident the remaining “hidden” biases were
minuscule. Thus there is no hope of measuring minuscule relative treatment effects with confidence in an
observational study.

Discussion
Textbook discussions of paired studies with binary outcomes often take a population sampling approach
(Fleiss et al., 2013). When inference is instead rooted in random assignment, it is often limited to testing the
null hypothesis of no treatment effect for any unit (Lehmann, 1975). This note has demonstrated how the
randomized treatment assignment provides the “reasoned basis for inference” for effect sizes. This leads to
different formulae than sampling-based derivations and is more defensible when units are not samples from
a population, when interest lies in the effect of treatment on the units in the study, or in an observational
study where hidden biases from unobserved factors may be present.
Previous work assumed monotonicity (Rosenbaum, 2002) or relied on an integer program solver (Rigdon
and Hudgens, 2015). The present note provides analytical formulae for point estimates, p-values (against
arbitrary null hypotheses), and large-sample approximations for confidence bounds (exact confidence bounds
in O(log S)) based on inverting tests. It also provides formulae for the interval summarizing sensitivity of
point estimates to hidden biases and expanded confidence intervals for observational studies.
When we know treatment effects are monotonic, incorporating this assumption achieves greater statistical
power and narrower confidence intervals. In many applications, this assumption is not defensible, and our
discussion shows it is unnecessary for inference.
